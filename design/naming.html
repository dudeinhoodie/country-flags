Naming study · second pass · English only

Ten Names, Ten Marks

The first pass was one idea run ten times: insider terms from vexillology, most of which need explaining before they mean anything. This pass takes ten different territories instead — the world, collecting, the moment of recognition, plain speech — so the choice is between directions rather than between synonyms.

The marks vary with them. A meridian is a circle, a postmark is a ring, a tricolour is nothing but colour. Ten icons that share a system but not a shape, because ten rectangles told apart only by their diagonals is not a set, it is a sheet.

Contact sheet

How they actually arrive: sixty pixels, on somebody’s third home screen, next to a bank and a messenger. Judge them here first.

The ten

01

Meridian

The world

A line of longitude. Every country on earth sits on one.

Puts the app where it actually lives — the whole globe — without saying globe. It is a grown-up word with no cartoon in it, it survives being said out loud in a room, and the mark is one arc: a sphere reduced to the single line that divides it.

What it risksUsed by banks, a hotel chain and a hedge fund. Common in business names, though rare in learning apps.

02

Orbis

The world

Latin for the world, and for the circle it is drawn as.

Short, hard, two syllables, and it sounds like it has existed for a while. The Latin does the work that a made-up name cannot: it means something exact rather than being assembled from pleasant sounds.

What it risksLatin names can read cold and slightly corporate. Check it does not sound like an insurance product to you.

03

Postmark

Collecting countries

The stamp a country puts on everything that leaves it.

Reframes the app as a collection rather than a test, which is closer to why anyone does this for months. Every country you learn is one more mark in the book, and the icon is that mark — a cancellation ring, the most country-specific object there is.

What it risksTwo syllables longer than the rest, and it points at post rather than at flags. Some will expect a mail app.

04

Onsight

The moment of knowing

From climbing: getting it first try, with no practice run.

Names the thing the app is actually training. You either know the flag the instant it appears or you do not, and that gap is the whole product. It is a real word from a real discipline, so it carries meaning rather than borrowing a vibe.

What it risksThe climbing sense is invisible to most people; they will read it as on-sight and think of eyewear.

05

Bandera

Flag, in another language

Flag — in Spanish, Italian and Portuguese.

The one name here that says exactly what the app is while still sounding like a name. Half a billion people read it instantly and everyone else hears something warm and open. It also quietly promises that this is about the whole world, not one corner of it.

What it risksPolitically loaded in Ukraine and Poland through the surname. That alone may rule it out.

06

Flagbook

Plain and findable

A field guide to flags, named the way a field guide is named.

The deliberate opposite of clever. Nobody needs it explained, it says flags in the first syllable, and it will be found by people typing what they want. Warm rather than technical, and it ages well because it never tried to be fashionable.

What it risksPlain to the point of forgettable, and close enough to Facebook’s naming to feel dated to some ears.

07

Tricolour

How flags are built

The three-band flag — the commonest design on earth.

More flags are tricolours than are anything else, so the name is a quiet fact about the subject rather than a metaphor. It looks handsome set in type, it is unmistakably European in the best way, and the icon needs no drawing at all: it is the thing itself.

What it risksLong, and the British spelling splits from the American. Pick one and lose the other’s search traffic.

08

Vane

Short and sharp

The small flag on a roof that shows which way things are going.

Four letters, one syllable, nothing to misspell. It is a flag that exists to be read at a distance, which is the skill, and it carries a second meaning about direction and progress without ever saying so.

What it risksA homophone of vain and vein, so it will be misheard and mistyped constantly.

09

Landfall

The moment of knowing

The first sight of land after a long crossing.

The feeling the app sells, named precisely: the second something unfamiliar resolves into somewhere you know. Nautical without being nautical-themed, and it has a shape to it — a beginning, not a category.

What it risksAlso the word for where a hurricane hits, which is the first result you will fight in search.

10

Colours

How flags are built

“The colours” has meant a nation’s flag for four hundred years.

An idiom rather than an object: to show your colours, to sail under false ones. It is the most human name on this list and the only one that could carry a brand beyond flags, into anything worth recognising at a glance.

What it risksA very common word, so the store listing and the domain will both be a fight. American spelling loses the elegance.

First pass, kept for comparison

Canton, Halyard, Ensign and the rest. All real flag terminology, all one idea — and three of them (Ensign, Semaphore, Pennant) are already busy elsewhere. Useful mainly as the thing the second pass is not.

If you want one answer

Meridian is the one I would build. It puts the app on the whole globe without saying globe, it is a grown-up word that survives being said in a meeting, the mark is a single arc that stays legible at twenty pixels, and nothing about it has to be explained to anyone. Its cost is company: plenty of businesses use it, though almost none of them are learning apps.

Postmark is the one I would build if the app is a collection rather than a test — and after a few months, it is. It reframes every country as a mark in a book instead of a question answered, which is a warmer promise and a longer one.

Colours is the outside bet. It is the only name here that could outgrow flags entirely, and the only one a person might say without knowing it was a product. It is also the hardest to own.

Still true from the first pass: none of this has been checked against the App Store or a trademark register, and that check is what turns a shortlist into a name.
